Monte Carlo Simulation • Layer 2: The Core Estimation Engine

The Law of Large Numbers, Central Limit Theorem, and Estimator Unbiasedness

Why does taking the arithmetic average of millions of computer-generated pseudo-random numbers yield the exact truth of a physical system? The entire edifice of Monte Carlo simulation rests on two asymptotic pillars: the Law of Large Numbers, which guarantees convergence, and the Central Limit Theorem, which dictates the rate and statistical shape of error. Yet, behind these textbook guarantees lurk critical engineering pitfalls: finite-sample variance bias, skewness-induced confidence interval collapse, and the sudden failure of convergence under heavy-tailed extremes.

In Layer 1 of this curriculum (MC 01 through MC 05), we engineered the algorithms required to synthesize non-uniform, bounded, and correlated stochastic variates on deterministic digital silicon.

Now, in Layer 2: The Core Estimation Engine & Asymptotics, we shift focus from the variates to the estimator itself. When we run $N$ simulation iterations to estimate expected bridge fatigue, hurricane storm surge delay, or power grid overload, what mathematical mechanisms protect us from error? Why does Monte Carlo achieve an error convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$ regardless of whether our model has two input parameters or two thousand? And when does this estimation engine catastrophically stall?

In this post, we unpack the mathematical mechanics of the Weak and Strong Laws of Large Numbers, prove estimator unbiasedness while uncovering the hidden Jensen’s inequality bias in sample standard error, derive the Lindeberg-Lévy Central Limit Theorem, quantify finite-sample non-normality via the Berry-Esseen Theorem, and stress-test an urban transit network resilience model under severe winter storm disruptions.

1. The Operational Dilemma: Convergence Guarantee vs. Finite-Sample Reality

Every Monte Carlo simulation attempts to compute an unknown multidimensional integral or expectation:

$$\theta = \mathbb{E}[g(\mathbf{X})] = \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} g(\mathbf{x}) f(\mathbf{x}) d\mathbf{x}$$

where $\mathbf{X} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a vector of random inputs with joint probability density function $f(\mathbf{x})$, and $g(\mathbf{x})$ is a deterministic system performance function (such as total network travel time, structural stress, or financial loss).

The standard Monte Carlo estimator $\hat{\theta}_N$ replaces the intractable continuous integral with an empirical arithmetic mean of $N$ independent, identically distributed (i.i.d.) realization evaluations:

$$\hat{\theta}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N g(\mathbf{X}_i), \quad \mathbf{X}_i \overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim} f(\mathbf{x})$$

In applied civil engineering, this formulation immediately triggers three critical operational questions:

  1. The Convergence Guarantee: Under what mathematical conditions are we guaranteed that as $N \to \infty$, the estimate $\hat{\theta}_N$ converges to the exact truth $\theta$?
  2. The Finite-Sample Accuracy: For a finite budget (say, $N = 5,000$ runs of an expensive finite element or microscopic traffic simulation), what is the expected error, and does our estimator introduce systematic bias?
  3. The Stopping Boundary: How can an engineer construct a mathematically rigorous confidence interval $[ \hat{\theta}_N - \epsilon, \hat{\theta}_N + \epsilon ]$ that guarantees a target precision with $95\%$ or $99\%$ certainty?

The answers are governed by two of the most celebrated theorems in probability theory: the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem.

2. Weak vs. Strong Law of Large Numbers

While engineers often refer to “the” Law of Large Numbers as a singular concept, probability theory provides two fundamentally distinct versions with different mathematical assumptions and convergence modes.

⚖️ WLLN vs. SLLN: Slice-Wise Probability Mass vs. Pathwise Confinement
Probability Geometry Sample-Path Dynamics ε-Corridor

Geometric distinction between the Weak Law (cross-sectional probability mass concentrating inside an ε-corridor at fixed N, while individual sample paths can still make future excursions) versus the Strong Law (almost sure pathwise trapping, guaranteeing every trajectory stays confined forever past index N0).

2.1 The Weak Law (Khinchin / Chebyshev)

Let $Y_i = g(\mathbf{X}_i)$ be independent and identically distributed random variables with finite expectation $\mathbb{E}[Y_i] = \theta$ and finite variance $\mathbb{V}[Y_i] = \sigma^2 < \infty$. The sample mean is:

$$\hat{\theta}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N Y_i$$

By linearity of expectation, the expected value of $\hat{\theta}_N$ is:

$$\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_N] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N Y_i \right] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[Y_i] = \frac{1}{N} (N \theta) = \theta$$

Because the realizations are statistically independent, the variance of the sum is the sum of the variances:

$$\mathbb{V}[\hat{\theta}_N] = \mathbb{V}\left[ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N Y_i \right] = \frac{1}{N^2} \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{V}[Y_i] = \frac{1}{N^2} (N \sigma^2) = \frac{\sigma^2}{N}$$

Now apply Chebyshev’s Inequality to the random variable $\hat{\theta}_N$ for any arbitrary positive tolerance $\epsilon > 0$:

$$\mathbb{P}\left( |\hat{\theta}_N - \theta| \ge \epsilon \right) \le \frac{\mathbb{V}[\hat{\theta}_N]}{\epsilon^2} = \frac{\sigma^2}{N \epsilon^2}$$

Taking the limit as the sample size $N$ approaches infinity:

$$\lim_{N \to \infty} \mathbb{P}\left( |\hat{\theta}_N - \theta| \ge \epsilon \right) \le \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{N \epsilon^2} = 0$$

This establishes convergence in probability ($\hat{\theta}_N \xrightarrow{P} \theta$): for any fixed error threshold $\epsilon$, the probability that the Monte Carlo estimator deviates by more than $\epsilon$ evaporates to zero as $N$ grows large.

2.2 The Strong Law (Kolmogorov)

While the Weak Law establishes that the cross-sectional probability mass outside $[ \theta - \epsilon, \theta + \epsilon ]$ vanishes at any fixed time step $N$, it does not guarantee that a single simulation path will never exit that boundary again in the future.

The Strong Law of Large Numbers (SLLN) provides this pathwise guarantee through almost sure convergence ($\hat{\theta}_N \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \theta$):

$$\mathbb{P}\left( \lim_{N \to \infty} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N g(\mathbf{X}_i) = \theta \right) = 1$$

Under Kolmogorov’s criterion, if the random variables $Y_i$ are i.i.d., the existence of a finite first moment $\mathbb{E}[|Y|] < \infty$ is both necessary and sufficient for the Strong Law to hold.

In operational terms: for any non-pathological engineering model with finite mean, if an engineer starts a simulation running on a server, that specific running sample path is mathematically guaranteed to enter the error band $[ \theta - \epsilon, \theta + \epsilon ]$ and remain trapped within it for all subsequent iterations $n \ge N_0$.

3. Estimator Unbiasedness and the Hidden Jensen Variance Gap

A foundational virtue of standard Monte Carlo simulation is that the estimator is unbiased for all finite sample sizes.

$$\text{Bias}(\hat{\theta}_N) = \mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_N] - \theta = 0, \quad \forall N \ge 1$$

Whether you run $N = 3$ iterations or $N = 3,000,000$, your estimator does not have a systematic directional skew. But what about our estimate of the error itself?

📐 Estimator Unbiasedness & The Jensen Standard Error Gap
Jensen's Inequality Finite-Sample Bias Standard Error

Proof of estimator unbiasedness contrasted against the strict downward bias of sample standard error SN. Because the square root function is strictly concave, Jensen's inequality guarantees that E[SN] < σ, underestimating true simulation uncertainty at small N.

3.1 Bessel’s Correction for Sample Variance

To measure the standard error of our estimator, we must estimate the population variance $\sigma^2 = \mathbb{V}[g(\mathbf{X})]$. The naive plug-in sample variance is:

$$\tilde{S}_N^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \hat{\theta}_N)^2$$

Computing its expectation reveals a systematic downward bias:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \hat{\theta}_N)^2 \right] &= \mathbb{E}\left[ \sum_{i=1}^N \left( (Y_i - \theta) - (\hat{\theta}_N - \theta) \right)^2 \right] \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^N \mathbb{E}[(Y_i - \theta)^2] - 2 \mathbb{E}\left[ (\hat{\theta}_N - \theta) \sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \theta) \right] + N \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta}_N - \theta)^2] \\ &= N \sigma^2 - 2 N \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta}_N - \theta)^2] + N \mathbb{E}[(\hat{\theta}_N - \theta)^2] \\ &= N \sigma^2 - N \left(\frac{\sigma^2}{N}\right) = (N - 1) \sigma^2 \end{aligned}$$

Dividing by $N$ produces $\mathbb{E}[\tilde{S}_N^2] = \frac{N-1}{N} \sigma^2 < \sigma^2$. Bessel’s correction divides by the degrees of freedom $N - 1$, producing the unbiased sample variance estimator:

$$S_N^2 = \frac{1}{N - 1} \sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \hat{\theta}_N)^2 \implies \mathbb{E}[S_N^2] = \sigma^2$$

3.2 Jensen’s Inequality and the Standard Error Bias

Engineers do not report variance; they report the standard error $\widehat{\text{SE}} = \frac{S_N}{\sqrt{N}} = \frac{\sqrt{S_N^2}}{\sqrt{N}}$.

Here lies a subtle statistical trap: taking the square root destroys unbiasedness.

Jensen’s Inequality states that for any non-degenerate random variable $W$ and any strictly concave function $f(w)$ where $f''(w) < 0$:

$$\mathbb{E}[f(W)] < f(\mathbb{E}[W])$$

Setting $W = S_N^2$ and $f(w) = \sqrt{w}$ (with $f''(w) = -\frac{1}{4} w^{-3/2} < 0$ for all $w > 0$):

$$\mathbb{E}[S_N] = \mathbb{E}\left[ \sqrt{S_N^2} \right] < \sqrt{\mathbb{E}[S_N^2]} = \sqrt{\sigma^2} = \sigma$$

Therefore:

$$\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\text{SE}}] < \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}$$

The estimated standard error is always systematically smaller than the true standard error on average! For Gaussian variates, the exact expectation is:

$$\mathbb{E}[S_N] = c_4(N) \cdot \sigma, \quad \text{where } c_4(N) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{N-1}} \frac{\Gamma(N/2)}{\Gamma((N-1)/2)}$$

4. The Central Limit Theorem and Dimensional Immunity

While the Law of Large Numbers establishes that $\hat{\theta}_N \to \theta$, it offers no information on the distribution of the error $\hat{\theta}_N - \theta$ for finite $N$. The Lindeberg-Lévy Central Limit Theorem (CLT) solves this completely.

🔔 CLT Asymptotic Normalization & Berry-Esseen Bound Topology
Lindeberg-Lévy CLT Berry-Esseen Theorem O(N^-1/2) Rate

Topological progression from an arbitrary skewed non-Gaussian density f(x) to the standardized Gaussian limit Φ(z). The Berry-Esseen theorem bounds the maximum vertical discrepancy Δ between the empirical distribution and the standard normal bell curve.

4.1 Theorem Formulation

Let $Y_1, Y_2, \dots, Y_N$ be independent, identically distributed random variables with mean $\theta = \mathbb{E}[Y_i]$ and strictly positive, finite variance $\sigma^2 = \mathbb{V}[Y_i] < \infty$. Then the standardized error distribution converges in distribution to the standard normal $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$:

$$Z_N = \frac{\hat{\theta}_N - \theta}{\sigma / \sqrt{N}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^N (Y_i - \theta)}{\sigma \sqrt{N}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } N \to \infty$$

Equivalently, the error distribution scales as:

$$\sqrt{N}(\hat{\theta}_N - \theta) \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$$

4.2 Proof Outline via Characteristic Functions

Let $W_i = \frac{Y_i - \theta}{\sigma}$ be the standardized zero-mean, unit-variance variates ($\mathbb{E}[W_i] = 0$, $\mathbb{E}[W_i^2] = 1$). The standardized sum is:

$$Z_N = \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \sum_{i=1}^N W_i$$

The characteristic function $\phi_W(t) = \mathbb{E}[e^{itW}]$ can be Taylor-expanded around $t = 0$:

$$\phi_W(t) = \mathbb{E}\left[ 1 + itW - \frac{t^2 W^2}{2} + o(t^2) \right] = 1 + 0 - \frac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2) = 1 - \frac{t^2}{2} + o(t^2)$$

Because the $W_i$ are independent, the characteristic function of $Z_N = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{W_i}{\sqrt{N}}$ is the product of individual characteristic functions evaluated at $t / \sqrt{N}$:

$$\phi_{Z_N}(t) = \left[ \phi_W\left( \frac{t}{\sqrt{N}} \right) \right]^N = \left[ 1 - \frac{t^2}{2N} + o\left(\frac{t^2}{N}\right) \right]^N$$

Using the fundamental limit $\lim_{N \to \infty} \left(1 + \frac{a}{N}\right)^N = e^a$:

$$\lim_{N \to \infty} \phi_{Z_N}(t) = \exp\left( -\frac{t^2}{2} \right)$$

This limit $\exp(-t^2/2)$ is precisely the characteristic function of a standard Gaussian random variable $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$. By Lévy’s Continuity Theorem, convergence of characteristic functions implies convergence in distribution ($Z_N \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1)$). $\blacksquare$

4.3 Dimensional Immunity: The Monte Carlo Superpower

Notice the most remarkable feature of the Central Limit Theorem result:

$$\text{Standard Error}(\hat{\theta}_N) = \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}} = \mathcal{O}\left(N^{-1/2}\right)$$

The integration dimension $d$ does not appear anywhere in this rate.

Whether our infrastructure simulation integrates over $d = 1$ dimension (e.g. single pavement thickness degradation) or $d = 1,000$ dimensions (e.g. dynamic traffic routing across a regional network with 1,000 stochastic link capacities), the standard error decays at exactly $N^{-1/2}$.

Contrast this with classical deterministic numerical integration (trapezoidal rule, Simpson’s rule, or Gauss-Legendre quadrature), whose error converges at rate:

$$\text{Quadrature Error} = \mathcal{O}\left( N^{-r / d} \right)$$

where $r$ is the smoothness of the integrand. In dimension $d = 8$, the trapezoidal rule achieves an abysmal convergence rate of $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/4})$. In dimension $d = 20$, it stalls completely. Monte Carlo’s dimensional immunity is the reason high-dimensional computational mechanics, quantum physics, and modern risk analysis are tractable.

4.4 The Berry-Esseen Bound: How Fast Does It Become Gaussian?

The CLT is an asymptotic theorem ($N \to \infty$). In practice, an engineer needs to know: is $N = 50$ large enough to assume the error is Gaussian?

The Berry-Esseen Theorem quantifies the worst-case deviation between the true cumulative distribution function $F_N(z) = \mathbb{P}(Z_N \le z)$ and the standard normal CDF $\Phi(z)$:

$$\sup_{z \in \mathbb{R}} \left| F_N(z) - \Phi(z) \right| \le \frac{C \cdot \rho_3}{\sigma^3 \sqrt{N}}$$

where:

  • $\rho_3 = \mathbb{E}[|Y - \theta|^3]$ is the third absolute central moment;
  • $\sigma = \sqrt{\mathbb{V}[Y]}$ is the population standard deviation;
  • $C$ is the universal Berry-Esseen constant (proven by Shevtsova in 2011 to be bounded by $C \le 0.4748$).

The ratio $\frac{\rho_3}{\sigma^3}$ is directly proportional to the skewness of the underlying distribution.

  • If the physical distribution is symmetric (e.g. Gaussian, Uniform), skewness is zero, $\rho_3/\sigma^3$ is small, and $N = 30$ produces near-perfect Gaussian confidence intervals.
  • If the distribution is heavily skewed (e.g. structural component failure times, extreme flood runoff, or traffic jam queues with lognormal tails), $\rho_3/\sigma^3$ can exceed $10$ or $20$. In such cases, $N = 100$ or $N = 500$ may still exhibit noticeable skewness, causing conventional two-sided $95\%$ confidence intervals to under-cover the heavy tail!

5. Pathological Breakdown & Infinite Moments

What happens when the mathematical prerequisites of the Law of Large Numbers and Central Limit Theorem are violated?

5.1 The Cauchy Catastrophe

The standard Cauchy distribution has probability density function:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\pi (1 + x^2)}, \quad x \in \mathbb{R}$$

Let us attempt to calculate its expected value:

$$\mathbb{E}[X] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{x}{\pi (1 + x^2)} dx = \lim_{R \to \infty} \left[ \frac{1}{2\pi} \ln(1 + x^2) \right]_{-R}^R$$

Because $\int_0^\infty \frac{x}{1+x^2} dx = \infty$ and $\int_{-\infty}^0 \frac{x}{1+x^2} dx = -\infty$, the integral $\infty - \infty$ is undefined. The Cauchy distribution has neither a mean nor a variance.

Now consider taking the sample mean of $N$ independent Cauchy draws:

$$\hat{\theta}_N = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$$

Using characteristic functions, the characteristic function of a single Cauchy variable is $\phi_X(t) = \exp(-|t|)$. The characteristic function of the sample mean is:

$$\phi_{\hat{\theta}_N}(t) = \left[ \phi_X\left( \frac{t}{N} \right) \right]^N = \left[ \exp\left( -\left| \frac{t}{N} \right| \right) \right]^N = \exp(-|t|)$$

The sample mean $\hat{\theta}_N$ has the exact same distribution as a single observation $X_1$!

Averaging 1,000,000 Cauchy variates provides zero variance reduction over taking a single sample. The running mean never converges; it continues to jump erratically across the entire real line forever (as demonstrated in Step 4 of our scrollytelling stage above).

5.2 Heavy-Tailed Pareto and Lévy Stable Distributions ($1 < \alpha < 2$)

In civil engineering, extreme financial losses, power grid cascading blackouts, and earthquake energy releases are frequently modeled by heavy-tailed Pareto distributions ($f(x) \propto x^{-(\alpha + 1)}$):

  1. If $\alpha \le 1$: Mean is infinite ($\mathbb{E}[X] = \infty$). Neither LLN nor CLT applies.
  2. If $1 < \alpha < 2$: Mean is finite ($\mathbb{E}[X] < \infty$), but variance is infinite ($\sigma^2 = \infty$).
    • The Strong Law holds: $\hat{\theta}_N \xrightarrow{\text{a.s.}} \theta$. The sample average will eventually converge to the true mean.
    • The standard CLT fails: The error does not scale as $N^{-1/2}$ and is not Gaussian. Instead, the Generalized Central Limit Theorem applies, and error converges to an $\alpha$-stable Lévy distribution at a degraded rate: $$\text{Error Rate} = \mathcal{O}\left( N^{-(1 - 1/\alpha)} \right)$$ For $\alpha = 1.2$, the convergence rate is $N^{-(1 - 1/1.2)} = N^{-0.167}$—roughly three times slower than standard Monte Carlo!

7. Civil Engineering Case Study: Transit Fleet Resilience under Winter Storm Shock

To anchor these concepts in professional civil and infrastructure engineering practice, consider estimating the total daily network passenger-hours of delay across an urban transit authority operating 12 arterial bus and light rail corridors during a winter weather event.

7.1 Problem Formulation

  1. Travel Demand: Passenger demand $D_k$ on corridor $k \in \{1, \dots, 12\}$ follows a lognormal distribution: $$D_k \sim \text{Lognormal}(\mu_D = 2.8, \sigma_D = 0.3) \quad (\text{hundreds of passengers/hr})$$
  2. Weather Vulnerability Shock: Stochastic snow accumulation and icy road friction reduce vehicle speed according to a Weibull hazard: $$W_k \sim \text{Weibull}(\text{shape } k = 2.2, \text{scale } \lambda = 1.2 \cdot S)$$ where $S \in [1, 10]$ is the regional Storm Severity Index.
  3. Corridor Delay Function: Delay per passenger on corridor $k$ follows an empirical congestion relationship: $$T_k = 12.0 + 6.0 \cdot \left[ 1 + 0.18 \left( \frac{W_k}{5.0} \right)^{3.2} \right] \quad (\text{minutes})$$
  4. Total System Delay: $$Y = \sum_{k=1}^{12} \frac{D_k \cdot T_k}{60.0} \quad (\text{passenger-hours of delay})$$

We wish to compute the expected system delay $\theta = \mathbb{E}[Y]$, establish a $95\%$ CLT confidence interval, verify coverage across $M = 10,000$ independent trials, and track Welford online variance accumulation.

7.2 Production Python Implementation

"""
Monte Carlo Simulation Curriculum - Post 06
Civil Infrastructure Case Study: Urban Transit Network Fleet Delay
Implements Welford Online Algorithm, CLT Asymptotic Confidence Intervals,
and Berry-Esseen Convergence Diagnostics.
"""

import numpy as np
from scipy import stats
import math

class WelfordAccumulator:
    """
    Computes numerically stable online running mean and sample variance
    in a single pass with O(1) memory overhead.
    """
    def __init__(self):
        self.n = 0
        self.mean = 0.0
        self.M2 = 0.0

    def update(self, x: float):
        self.n += 1
        delta = x - self.mean
        self.mean += delta / self.n
        delta2 = x - self.mean
        self.M2 += delta * delta2

    @property
    def variance(self) -> float:
        return self.M2 / (self.n - 1) if self.n > 1 else 0.0

    @property
    def std_dev(self) -> float:
        return math.sqrt(self.variance)

    @property
    def standard_error(self) -> float:
        return self.std_dev / math.sqrt(self.n) if self.n > 0 else 0.0

def simulate_transit_system(storm_severity: float = 5.0, rng=None) -> float:
    """
    Evaluates one realization of the 12-corridor urban transit network.
    Returns total passenger-hours of system delay.
    """
    if rng is None:
        rng = np.random.default_rng()

    # 12 Corridors
    # Demand: Lognormal(2.8, 0.3)
    demands = rng.lognormal(mean=2.8, sigma=0.3, size=12)

    # Weather Shock: Weibull(shape=2.2, scale=1.2 * S)
    weibull_samples = rng.weibull(a=2.2, size=12)
    weather_shock = (1.2 * storm_severity) * weibull_samples

    # Corridor Delay (minutes per passenger)
    corridor_delays = 12.0 + 6.0 * (1.0 + 0.18 * np.power(weather_shock / 5.0, 3.2))

    # Total System Delay in Hours
    total_passenger_hours = np.sum(demands * corridor_delays) / 60.0
    return float(total_passenger_hours)

def run_monte_carlo_experiment(
    N: int = 2000, 
    storm_severity: float = 5.0, 
    confidence: float = 0.95,
    seed: int = 42
):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    accumulator = WelfordAccumulator()
    delays = np.empty(N, dtype=np.float64)

    for i in range(N):
        y = simulate_transit_system(storm_severity, rng)
        accumulator.update(y)
        delays[i] = y

    # Compute CLT bounds
    z_crit = stats.norm.ppf(1.0 - (1.0 - confidence) / 2.0)
    mean_est = accumulator.mean
    se_est = accumulator.standard_error
    ci_half_width = z_crit * se_est
    ci_lower = mean_est - ci_half_width
    ci_upper = mean_est + ci_half_width

    # Higher-moment diagnostics
    skewness = float(stats.skew(delays))
    kurtosis = float(stats.kurtosis(delays)) # excess kurtosis

    # Berry-Esseen Upper Bound Estimate
    rho3 = np.mean(np.abs(delays - mean_est)**3)
    sigma3 = accumulator.std_dev**3
    berry_esseen_bound = (0.4748 * rho3) / (sigma3 * np.sqrt(N))

    print("=" * 65)
    print(f" URBAN TRANSIT FLEET RELIABILITY SIMULATION (N = {N:,})")
    print(f" Storm Severity Index: Level {storm_severity}")
    print("=" * 65)
    print(f" Expected System Delay (Point Estimate): {mean_est:.2f} passenger-hours")
    print(f" Sample Standard Deviation (S):          {accumulator.std_dev:.2f} hrs")
    print(f" Standard Error (SE = S / sqrt(N)):      {se_est:.3f} hrs")
    print(f" {int(confidence*100)}% Asymptotic CLT Confidence Interval:  [{ci_lower:.2f}, {ci_upper:.2f}] hrs")
    print(f" Precision Relative Half-Width (&epsilon; / &theta;):     &plusmn;{(ci_half_width / mean_est)*100:.2f}%")
    print("-" * 65)
    print(" MOMENT & CONVERGENCE DIAGNOSTICS:")
    print(f" Sample Skewness (&gamma;_1):                     {skewness:.3f}")
    print(f" Sample Excess Kurtosis (&kappa;):                {kurtosis:.3f}")
    print(f" Berry-Esseen Theoretical Bound:         &Delta; &le; {berry_esseen_bound:.4f}")
    print("=" * 65)

    return delays, accumulator

8. Layer 2 Trajectory & Bridge to Post 07

In this post, we established the foundational laws of the Monte Carlo engine:

  1. Unbiasedness: The Monte Carlo point estimator is strictly unbiased ($\mathbb{E}[\hat{\theta}_N] = \theta$) for any finite sample size $N \ge 1$.
  2. Standard Error Bias: Taking the square root of sample variance introduces a strict downward Jensen bias ($\mathbb{E}[S_N] < \sigma$), understating uncertainty in small pilot runs ($N < 30$).
  3. Law of Large Numbers: Guarantees that the empirical average converges to the true population expectation, provided the first moment exists ($\mathbb{E}[|X|] < \infty$).
  4. Central Limit Theorem: Guarantees that estimator error converges to a normal distribution scaling at $\mathcal{O}(N^{-1/2})$, immune to the problem dimension $d$.
  5. Berry-Esseen Bound: Dictates that parent distribution skewness delays the onset of Gaussianity, requiring larger sample sizes for heavily skewed infrastructure hazards.
