Monte Carlo Simulation • Layer 1: Foundations

Pseudo-Random Number Generators: Entropy, Periodicity, and Seed Hygiene

In 1951, John von Neumann famously remarked: "Anyone who considers arithmetical methods of producing random digits is, of course, in a state of sin." Computers are strictly deterministic finite-state machines; they cannot roll dice. Yet modern scientific computing hinges entirely on deterministic sequences that mimic the statistical properties of genuine entropy. When an engineer selects an outdated or flawed generator, the simulation silently collapses into low-dimensional hyperplanes—producing false correlations and corrupted structural risk assessments.

Every Monte Carlo simulation begins with a single operational primitive: a generator that produces a sequence of independent, identically distributed uniform variates $U_1, U_2, U_3, \dots \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$. If this foundation is cracked, every subsequent calculation—rejection sampling, variance reduction, and Markov chain convergence—is invalid.

Crucially, in scientific simulation, determinism is not a defect; it is our greatest asset. True physical randomness (such as thermal noise or radioactive decay) cannot be rewound. Without reproducible pseudo-random sequences, we cannot debug stochastic algorithms, perform sensitivity analysis using Common Random Numbers (CRN), or reproduce published engineering risk studies. The goal of modern pseudo-random number generator (PRNG) design is not to eliminate determinism, but to engineer periods so astronomical, and state spaces so uniform, that no finite computation can distinguish the sequence from true entropy.

Step 2

The RANDU Catastrophe (1968)

During the 1960s, IBM distributed RANDU as the default random number generator across its mainframe System/360 architecture:

$$ X_{n+1} = (65539 \cdot X_n) \pmod{2^{31}} $$

In one and two dimensions, RANDU appeared perfectly uniform. But in 1968, mathematician George Marsaglia discovered an astonishing mathematical flaw: because $65539 = 2^{16} + 3$, consecutive triplets satisfy the exact linear recurrence:

$$ 9X_k - 6X_{k+1} + X_{k+2} \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{31}} $$

Watch the stage: when the camera rotates to Marsaglia's critical viewing angle, the entire 3D point cloud collapses into merely 15 parallel two-dimensional planes! Between these planes lie enormous empty voids where no simulated particle can ever land.

Step 3

Marsaglia's Theorem: Hyperplane Bounds

Marsaglia proved that the RANDU collapse was not an isolated glitch; it is an intrinsic geometric property of all Linear Congruential Generators.

$$ k \le (n! \cdot m)^{1/n} $$

In $n$-dimensional space, all $n$-tuples from any LCG with modulus $m$ are confined to at most $k$ parallel hyperplanes. For a 32-bit generator in 3 dimensions:

$$ k \le (3! \cdot 2^{31})^{1/3} \approx 2{,}344 \text{ planes} $$

In $n = 10$ dimensions (common in structural reliability and multi-barrier transport), $k \le 41$ hyperplanes! The stage renders these planar boundaries intersecting the cube, demonstrating why high-dimensional simulations break down when powered by primitive generators.

Step 4

Modern Space-Filling: NumPy PCG64

Modern scientific computing has abandoned raw linear generators. NumPy's default generator is PCG64 (Permuted Congruential Generator), designed by Melissa O'Neill in 2014.

PCG64 pairs a 128-bit linear state transition with a non-linear bitwise permutation output function. Its state space is astronomical ($2^{128} \approx 3.4 \times 10^{38}$), and its theoretical hyperplane bound in 3D exceeds $10^{18}$.

Rotate the stage in any direction: 2,000 points fill the volume uniformly with zero visible lattice degradation. Furthermore, PCG64 supports $2^{63}$ mathematically independent streams, making it safe for massive parallel clusters.

1. Linear Congruential Generators & Hull-Dobell Conditions

The simplest and most historically widespread class of PRNGs is the Linear Congruential Generator (LCG), introduced by D. H. Lehmer in 1949. An LCG is defined by the first-order modular recurrence relation:

Linear Congruential Generator Recurrence

$$ X_{n+1} = (a X_n + c) \pmod m $$ $$ U_n = \frac{X_n}{m} \in [0, 1) $$

Where:

  • $m > 0$ is the modulus (determines the maximum possible state space);
  • $a \in (0, m)$ is the multiplier;
  • $c \in [0, m)$ is the additive increment (if $c = 0$, it is termed a multiplicative congruential generator or Lehmer generator);
  • $X_0 \in [0, m)$ is the seed or initial state.

Because the state $X_n$ is confined to the finite set $\{0, 1, \dots, m-1\}$, the Pigeonhole Principle dictates that the sequence must eventually repeat. The length of the sequence before it repeats is its period $P \le m$.

The Hull-Dobell Theorem (1962)

A common pitfall among practitioners is assuming that choosing a large $m$ guarantees a long period. In reality, poorly selected parameters cause the generator to degenerate into short, closed sub-cycles that visit only a tiny fraction of the states.

In 1962, T. E. Hull and A. R. Dobell established the necessary and sufficient conditions for an LCG to achieve its maximal full period $P = m$:

Hull-Dobell Theorem: An LCG has full period $m$ for all seed values $X_0$ if and only if:
  1. $c$ and $m$ are relatively prime (coprime): $\gcd(c, m) = 1$;
  2. $a - 1$ is divisible by every prime factor $p$ of $m$;
  3. $a - 1$ is divisible by $4$ if $m$ is divisible by $4$.

If any of these conditions fails, the state space fragments into disjoint trap cycles. For example, if $m = 2^{32}$ (a standard 32-bit integer register), condition 2 requires that $a - 1$ be even, and condition 3 requires that $a - 1$ be a multiple of 4 (i.e. $a \equiv 1 \pmod 4$).

2. Marsaglia’s Hyperplane Theorem & The RANDU Collapse

Even when an LCG satisfies the Hull-Dobell conditions and achieves a full period $P = m$, it possesses a fatal structural vulnerability known as the spectral lattice defect.

In 1968, George Marsaglia published a landmark paper titled “Random Numbers Fall Mainly in the Plains”, proving that points generated by linear congruential generators form regular crystalline lattices in multi-dimensional space.

Marsaglia's Hyperplane Upper Bound

If an LCG is used to construct vectors of $n$ consecutive random numbers $(U_{k}, U_{k+1}, \dots, U_{k+n-1})$ in the unit hypercube $[0, 1]^n$, all points lie on at most $k$ parallel $(n-1)$-dimensional hyperplanes, bounded by: $$ k \le (n! \cdot m)^{1/n} $$

Derivation of the RANDU Catastrophe

To understand how severely this affects simulations, consider the exact parameters chosen by IBM for RANDU: $m = 2^{31}$, $a = 65539 = 2^{16} + 3$, and $c = 0$.

Notice the algebraic property of the multiplier:

$$ a = 2^{16} + 3 \implies a^2 = (2^{16} + 3)^2 = 2^{32} + 6 \cdot 2^{16} + 9 $$

Now examine three consecutive states $X_k, X_{k+1}, X_{k+2}$ generated by RANDU:

$$ X_{k+1} = a X_k \pmod{2^{31}} $$

$$ X_{k+2} = a X_{k+1} = a^2 X_k \pmod{2^{31}} $$

Substitute $a^2$:

$$ X_{k+2} \equiv (2^{32} + 6 \cdot 2^{16} + 9) X_k \pmod{2^{31}} $$

Since $2^{32} X_k \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{31}}$:

$$ X_{k+2} \equiv (6 \cdot 2^{16} + 9) X_k \pmod{2^{31}} $$

We can rewrite $6 \cdot 2^{16}$ in terms of $a = 2^{16} + 3$:

$$ 6a = 6(2^{16} + 3) = 6 \cdot 2^{16} + 18 \implies 6 \cdot 2^{16} = 6a - 18 $$

Substituting this back gives:

$$ X_{k+2} \equiv (6a - 18 + 9) X_k \equiv (6a - 9) X_k \pmod{2^{31}} $$

$$ X_{k+2} \equiv 6(a X_k) - 9 X_k \equiv 6 X_{k+1} - 9 X_k \pmod{2^{31}} $$

Rearranging yields the catastrophic linear relationship:

$$ X_{k+2} - 6 X_{k+1} + 9 X_k \equiv 0 \pmod{2^{31}} $$

Dividing by the modulus $2^{31}$ to convert to normalized uniform variates $U_k = X_k / 2^{31}$:

$$ 9 U_k - 6 U_{k+1} + U_{k+2} = c $$

where $c$ can only take integer values. Because $U \in [0, 1]$, the term $9 U_k - 6 U_{k+1} + U_{k+2}$ is bounded between $-6$ and $10$. Consequently, $c$ can assume at most 15 integer values ($\{-5, -4, \dots, 9\}$)!

Every single triplet generated by RANDU lies on one of these 15 parallel two-dimensional planes inside the 3D unit cube. If an engineer used RANDU to model 3D molecular dynamics, urban traffic intersections, or structural frames, the simulated particles literally lived inside a 15-layer pancake!

3. Beyond LCGs: Mersenne Twister vs PCG64

To escape the dimensional traps of LCGs, computational mathematics underwent a generational shift toward two distinct architectures: Linear Feedback Shift Registers (LFSR) and Permuted Congruential Generators (PCG).

1. Lehmer LCG (1949)

32-bit state; period $P \le 2^{32}$. Severe hyperplane banding ($k \le 2344$). Obsolete for scientific work.

2. Mersenne Twister (1998)

Twisted GFSR with period $2^{19937}-1$. 623-dimension equidistributed. Standard in Python/R/MATLAB.

3. PCG64 (2014)

128-bit state + non-linear bit permutations. Passes TestU01 BigCrush; supports $2^{63}$ disjoint parallel streams.

4. Philox4x64 (2011)

Stateless cryptographic counter generator. Unbounded periods; ideal for GPU kernels and MPI clusters.

The Mersenne Twister (MT19937)

Developed by Makoto Matsumoto and Takuji Nishimura in 1998, the Mersenne Twister (MT19937) was designed to address every practical limitation of LCGs:

  • Astronomical Period: $P = 2^{19937} - 1 \approx 4.3 \times 10^{6001}$ (a Mersenne prime).
  • High-Dimensional Equidistribution: Mathematically proven to be equidistributed in up to 623 dimensions for 32-bit accuracy.
  • Speed: Built entirely on fast bitwise shift, XOR, and mask operations without expensive integer division.

Despite being the default engine in Python (random), R, MATLAB, and Julia for over two decades, MT19937 has serious modern vulnerabilities:

  1. Bulky Internal State: MT requires a 624-word state vector (2,496 bytes). In parallel workloads with millions of independent threads (e.g. GPU simulations), this memory footprint wastes cache lines.
  2. Slow Zero Recovery (“Zero-Funneling”): If seeded with a state containing many zeros, MT can require up to 800,000 draws before producing statistically balanced output.
  3. Failure in Modern Test Batteries: MT19937 fails several linear complexity tests in the rigorous TestU01 BigCrush suite (MatrixRank, LinearComp), because its output is a strictly linear transformation over the Galois field $\mathbb{F}_2$.

The Permuted Congruential Generator (PCG64)

In 2014, Melissa O’Neill introduced the PCG family. The core philosophical insight was simple: LCGs have excellent, mathematically proven state transitions, but flawed linear output mappings. By separating the state transition from the output function, we can retain simplicity while destroying linearity.

PCG Architecture: Linear State + Non-Linear Permutation

$$ \text{State Transition (128-bit LCG)}: \quad S_{n+1} = (a S_n + c) \pmod{2^{128}} $$ $$ \text{Output Permutation (XSL-RR)}: \quad U_n = \text{rotate\_right}\Big( (S_n \oplus (S_n \gg 64)) \gg \text{rot}, \text{rot} \Big) $$

By applying bitwise XOR shifts and random rotations parameterized by high-order bits of the state, PCG destroys the crystalline hyperplanes of the LCG. PCG64:

  • Has a tiny 128-bit state (16 bytes);
  • Passes all TestU01 BigCrush and PractRand tests with zero failures;
  • Executes faster than MT19937 on modern x86/ARM hardware;
  • Natively supports $2^{63}$ independent streams by altering the additive increment $c$.

In NumPy 1.17+, PCG64 replaced MT19937 as the default generator in numpy.random.default_rng().

4. Parallel Seed Hygiene & The Birthday Collision Trap

In modern high-performance computing (HPC), Monte Carlo simulations are distributed across hundreds of CPU cores or thousands of GPU threads. The most dangerous engineering mistake in parallel simulation is uncoordinated seed initialization.

The Fatal Anti-Pattern: seed = base + worker_id

A pervasive bug in engineering scripts looks like this:

# FATAL ANTI-PATTERN: DO NOT USE
import multiprocessing as mp

def run_worker(rank):
    # Initializing worker with simple rank offset
    np.random.seed(12345 + rank)
    return simulate_bridge_network()

When an LCG or linear generator is initialized with sequential seeds $S_0, S_0+1, S_0+2$, the initial states are separated by a constant delta. Because the state transition is linear, the trajectories of different workers are correlated in phase: Worker 2 simply repeats Worker 1’s random numbers shifted by a few iterations!

The Birthday Collision Trap

What if we instead draw seeds uniformly at random from a 32-bit state space ($P = 2^{31} \approx 2.15 \times 10^9$)?

Suppose we deploy $W$ parallel workers, each drawing $K$ random numbers. A catastrophic collision occurs if the trajectory of Worker $B$ overlaps with the trajectory of Worker $A$. By the generalized Birthday Problem, the probability of at least one trajectory overlap across $W$ workers is:

$$ P(\text{Trajectory Overlap}) \approx 1 - \exp\left( - \frac{W(W - 1) \cdot K}{2 \cdot P} \right) $$

Let us evaluate this for a standard civil engineering reliability run:

  • $W = 64$ parallel CPU cores;
  • $K = 10^6$ Monte Carlo samples per core;
  • Using a standard 32-bit generator ($P = 2^{31} \approx 2.15 \times 10^9$).
$$ \frac{W(W-1)K}{2P} = \frac{64 \times 63 \times 10^6}{2 \times 2.147 \times 10^9} \approx \frac{4.032 \times 10^9}{4.295 \times 10^9} \approx 0.9388 $$

$$ P(\text{Collision}) \approx 1 - e^{-0.9388} \approx 1 - 0.391 = \mathbf{60.9\%} $$

There is a greater than 60% probability that two of your parallel workers simulate duplicate, overlapping trajectories! When sequences overlap, the combined variance does not decrease by $1/\sqrt{W \cdot K}$; the effective sample size is halved, and the resulting confidence intervals are falsely narrow.

The Modern Solution: SeedSequence.spawn()

To guarantee statistical independence without risk of overlap, modern NumPy uses the SeedSequence protocol. Rather than picking scalar integers, SeedSequence uses a 128-bit entropy pool and a cryptographic hashing algorithm (splitmix64) to spawn provably disjoint, mathematically independent PRNG streams:

from numpy.random import SeedSequence, default_rng
import concurrent.futures

# 1. Master seed sequence (contains 128 bits of entropy)
master_seed = SeedSequence(9876543210123456789)

# 2. Spawn 64 independent child seeds
n_workers = 64
child_seeds = master_seed.spawn(n_workers)

def worker_task(seed):
    # Each worker instantiates an independent PCG64 instance
    rng = default_rng(seed)
    return rng.random(1000)

with concurrent.futures.ProcessPoolExecutor() as executor:
    results = list(executor.map(worker_task, child_seeds))

5. Engineering Case Study: Regional Bridge Network Seismic Reliability

To see how PRNG flaws compromise infrastructure decisions, consider evaluating the post-earthquake connectivity of a regional transportation network consisting of 50 highway bridges.

Physical Model Formulation

During a major seismic event, ground motion attenuates with distance from the fault rupture. According to ground-motion prediction equations (GMPEs), the Peak Ground Acceleration (PGA) at bridge $i$ located at distance $R_i$ is modeled lognormally:

Ground Motion & Structural Fragility Formulation

$$ \ln(\text{PGA}_i) = \ln(\mu_{\text{PGA}}(M, R_i)) + \varepsilon_i, \quad \varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2) $$ $$ P(\text{Collapse}_i \mid \text{PGA}_i) = \Phi\left( \frac{\ln(\text{PGA}_i) - \lambda_i}{\zeta_i} \right) $$

Where $\lambda_i$ and $\zeta_i$ are structural median capacity and dispersion parameters, and $\Phi(\cdot)$ is the standard normal CDF. The network fails if a key critical cut-set of bridges collapses simultaneously, severing the emergency evacuation route.

The Catastrophic Effect of Flawed Seeding

If the 50 bridges are evaluated using parallel workers that suffer from PRNG cross-correlation (due to seed = base + worker_id or 32-bit state overlap):

  1. Artificial Spatial Synchronization: Residuals $\varepsilon_i$ that should be independent become phase-locked across adjacent bridge calculations.
  2. Gross Risk Overestimation: Simultaneous multi-bridge failures occur with spuriously high frequency, causing the simulation to overestimate the network isolation probability by over 300%!

The following production-ready Python script demonstrates how to execute this simulation safely using NumPy’s SeedSequence.spawn() and parallel thread execution:

"""
Regional Highway Bridge Network Seismic Reliability Simulator
Demonstrates safe parallel PRNG stream spawning with NumPy SeedSequence.
"""

import numpy as np
from numpy.random import SeedSequence, default_rng
from concurrent.futures import ThreadPoolExecutor

def simulate_bridge_network_batch(seed_seq, n_sims, n_bridges=50):
    """
    Simulates seismic collapse reliability for a network of bridges.
    Guaranteed independent stream using SeedSequence.
    """
    rng = default_rng(seed_seq)
    
    # Bridge distance from seismic epicenter (km)
    distances = np.linspace(5, 60, n_bridges)
    
    # Median ground motion attenuation (Campbell-Bozorgnia style)
    # ln(PGA_median) = 0.5 * M - 1.2 * ln(R + 5)
    mag = 7.2
    ln_pga_median = 0.5 * mag - 1.2 * np.log(distances + 5.0)
    sigma_pga = 0.45  # Intra-event ground motion dispersion
    
    # Structural capacity parameters: median ln(capacity) and log-dispersion
    cap_median = np.linspace(0.65, 0.95, n_bridges)  # in g (acceleration)
    cap_zeta = 0.35
    
    # Generate ground motion realizations: (n_sims, n_bridges)
    # Independent draws across bridges and simulations
    eps = rng.standard_normal((n_sims, n_bridges))
    pga_realizations = np.exp(ln_pga_median + sigma_pga * eps)
    
    # Probability of collapse for each bridge: Phi((ln(pga) - ln(cap)) / zeta)
    from scipy.special import ndtr
    p_collapse = ndtr((np.log(pga_realizations) - np.log(cap_median)) / cap_zeta)
    
    # Monte Carlo collapse state: Bernoulli trial
    u_collapse = rng.random((n_sims, n_bridges))
    bridge_failed = u_collapse < p_collapse
    
    # Network Failure Criterion: Route severed if >= 4 critical bridges collapse
    route_severed = np.sum(bridge_failed, axis=1) >= 4
    
    return np.mean(route_severed)

def main():
    total_sims = 1_000_000
    n_workers = 8
    sims_per_worker = total_sims // n_workers
    
    # Initialize cryptographic master entropy
    master_seed = SeedSequence(0x9E3779B97F4A7C15)
    worker_seeds = master_seed.spawn(n_workers)
    
    print(f"Running {total_sims:,} parallel seismic network simulations across {n_workers} streams...")
    
    with ThreadPoolExecutor(max_workers=n_workers) as executor:
        futures = [
            executor.submit(simulate_bridge_network_batch, s, sims_per_worker)
            for s in worker_seeds
        ]
        results = [f.result() for f in futures]
    
    p_failure_est = np.mean(results)
    std_error = np.std(results, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n_workers)
    
    print(f"Estimated Network Failure Probability: {p_failure_est:.5f}")
    print(f"Standard Error: {std_error:.6f}")
    print(f"95% Confidence Interval: [{p_failure_est - 1.96*std_error:.5f}, {p_failure_est + 1.96*std_error:.5f}]")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

6. Synthesis & Method Selection Matrix

When choosing a pseudo-random engine for scientific simulation, prioritize period length, dimensional equidistribution, and parallel stream capabilities over raw CPU speed.

PRNG Architecture State Size Period Equidistribution TestU01 BigCrush Recommended Application
Linear Congruential (LCG) 32 / 48 bits $\le 2^{48}$ Fails in $\ge 3$D (Marsaglia) Fails catastrophically Never use for research
Mersenne Twister (MT19937) 2,496 bytes $2^{19937}-1$ 623 dimensions Fails 2 linear rank tests Legacy single-threaded CPU scripts
Permuted Congruential (PCG64) 16 bytes (128-bit) $2^{128}$ Full uniform space-filling Passes all tests Modern default for scientific computing
Counter-Based (Philox4x64-10) 32 bytes $2^{256}$ Cryptographic diffusion Passes all tests GPU kernels (CUDA / JAX), MPI clusters
