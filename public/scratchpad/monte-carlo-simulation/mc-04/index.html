Acceptance-Rejection Sampling: Geometry, Bounding Envelopes, and Efficiency

In 1951, while computing neutron diffusion trajectories at Los Alamos, John von Neumann confronted a fundamental impasse: physical probability distributions frequently possess cumulative distribution functions that cannot be integrated in closed form or inverted analytically. His solution was brilliant in its geometric simplicity: If you cannot directly sample along the curve of a target distribution, enclose it within an easily sampled bounding envelope, throw random darts uniformly beneath the envelope, and accept only those darts that land underneath the target density.

Acceptance-Rejection sampling is the universal computational fallback of computational statistics. It allows engineers to draw exact variates from arbitrary densities—including Beta material quality ratings, Gamma vehicle headways, truncated Gaussian stress limits, and unnormalized Bayesian posterior distributions—without ever computing an analytical inverse.

Yet this geometric flexibility carries a physical cost: every rejected dart represents wasted computational cycles and discarded entropy. In this article, we dissect the mechanics of bounding envelopes, prove exact mathematical convergence, examine why fat-tailed distributions break naive proposals, explore George Marsaglia’s squeeze acceleration technique, and apply the method to calibrate freeway vehicle headways from real-world loop detector data.

Unbounded Domains: Half-Normal via Exponential Envelope

When the target variable is unbounded on $[0, \infty)$—such as the Half-Normal distribution $X \sim \mathcal{N}^+(0, 1)$—a flat uniform envelope is mathematically impossible because a uniform distribution cannot integrate to 1 over an infinite domain.

Instead, we employ an Exponential proposal $q(x) = e^{-x}$. By solving the calculus optimization problem $\sup [p(x)/q(x)]$, we obtain the exact optimal multiplier $c = \sqrt{2e/\pi} \approx 1.3155$.

Notice how tightly the exponential envelope drapes over the Gaussian bell. The acceptance efficiency surges to $76.0\%$, requiring only an average of $1.32$ proposals per accepted sample.

Step 4

The Dimensional Wall: The Curse of Dimensionality

Why cannot we use Acceptance-Rejection to sample joint distributions in high-dimensional machine learning or 50-variable finite element reliability models?

Consider inscribing a $d$-dimensional hypersphere of radius $1$ inside a bounding hypercube of width $2$. In $d = 1$, acceptance is $2/2 = 100\%$. In $d = 2$, it is $\pi/4 \approx 78.5\%$. In $d = 10$, the volume ratio collapses to $\frac{\pi^5 / 120}{2^{10}} \approx 0.25\%$.

In $d = 50$, the acceptance rate is approximately $1.5 \times 10^{-28}$. You would have to wait longer than the age of the universe to accept a single point. This catastrophic exponential collapse is why high-dimensional modeling demands Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC).

1. The Geometry and Mathematical Theory of Rejection Sampling

Suppose we wish to sample a continuous random variable $X \in \mathbb{R}$ governed by a target probability density function $p(x)$. We assume that:

  1. Direct inversion of the cumulative distribution function $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x p(t)dt$ is computationally prohibitive or analytically impossible.
  2. We possess a convenient, easily sampled proposal distribution $q(x)$ whose support completely covers the support of $p(x)$ (that is, $q(x) > 0$ whenever $p(x) > 0$).
  3. There exists a finite bounding scalar $c \ge 1$ such that:
$$ p(x) \le c \cdot q(x), \quad \forall x \in \text{supp}(p) $$

The quantity $c \cdot q(x)$ forms a majorizing bounding envelope over the target curve $p(x)$.

Geometric Mechanics: The Majorizing Bounding Envelope

Candidate proposals $Y \sim q(y)$ with uniform vertical coordinates $V = U \cdot c \cdot q(Y)$. Darts landing beneath target density $p(Y)$ are accepted; darts in the upper envelope gap are discarded as computational waste.

The Acceptance-Rejection Algorithm

The sampling procedure executes in three distinct steps:

  1. Draw a Proposal Candidate: Generate a random candidate $Y \sim q(y)$ (for example, using the Inverse Transform Method from Post 03).
  2. Draw a Uniform Test Coordinate: Generate an independent standard uniform random variable $U \sim \mathcal{U}(0, 1)$, establishing a vertical coordinate $V = U \cdot c \cdot q(Y)$.
  3. Evaluate the Acceptance Test:
    • If $U \le \frac{p(Y)}{c \cdot q(Y)}$ (or equivalently, $V \le p(Y)$), ACCEPT the candidate and set $X = Y$.
    • Otherwise, REJECT $Y$ and return to Step 1.

Proof of Correctness: Why Does the Output Exactly Follow $p(x)$?

To prove that the accepted variates faithfully adhere to the target distribution, we must show that the conditional cumulative distribution function of accepted proposals $P(Y \le x \mid \text{Accept})$ is identical to the target CDF $F(x) = \int_{-\infty}^x p(t)dt$.

By the definition of conditional probability:

$$ P(Y \le x \mid \text{Accept}) = \frac{P\left( Y \le x \text{ and } U \le \frac{p(Y)}{c \cdot q(Y)} \right)}{P\left( U \le \frac{p(Y)}{c \cdot q(Y)} \right)} $$

Let us evaluate the joint probability in the numerator by conditioning on the value of $Y = y$:

$$ P\left( Y \le x, U \le \frac{p(Y)}{c \cdot q(Y)} \right) = \int_{-\infty}^x \left[ \int_0^{\frac{p(y)}{c \cdot q(y)}} 1 \, du \right] q(y) \, dy $$

Evaluating the inner integral over $u$:

$$ \int_0^{\frac{p(y)}{c \cdot q(y)}} 1 \, du = \frac{p(y)}{c \cdot q(y)} $$

Substituting this back into the outer integral:

$$ \int_{-\infty}^x \left( \frac{p(y)}{c \cdot q(y)} \right) q(y) \, dy = \frac{1}{c} \int_{-\infty}^x p(y) \, dy = \frac{F(x)}{c} $$

Now, evaluate the denominator, which is the unconditional probability of accepting a proposal on any given trial:

$$ P(\text{Accept}) = P\left( U \le \frac{p(Y)}{c \cdot q(Y)} \right) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{p(y)}{c \cdot q(y)} q(y) \, dy = \frac{1}{c} \int_{-\infty}^\infty p(y) \, dy = \frac{1}{c} \cdot 1 = \frac{1}{c} $$

Taking the ratio of the numerator to the denominator:

$$ P(Y \le x \mid \text{Accept}) = \frac{F(x) / c}{1 / c} = F(x) \quad \blacksquare $$

The conditioning cancels $c$ completely! Regardless of the shape of the proposal envelope $q(x)$ or the size of $c$, the accepted points follow the target distribution with absolute mathematical precision.

The Distribution of Trials: How Much Work Does It Take?

Each trial in the algorithm is an independent Bernoulli trial with success probability $p_{\text{acc}} = \frac{1}{c}$. Consequently, the number of candidate proposals $T$ required to generate a single accepted variate follows a Geometric distribution:

$$ P(T = k) = \left(1 - \frac{1}{c}\right)^{k-1} \left(\frac{1}{c}\right), \quad k \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots\} $$

The fundamental properties of this trial distribution govern the computational runtime:

  • Expected Proposals per Sample: $$ \mathbb{E}[T] = \frac{1}{p_{\text{acc}}} = c $$
  • Variance of Proposals per Sample: $$ \text{Var}(T) = \frac{1 - p_{\text{acc}}}{p_{\text{acc}}^2} = c(c - 1) $$
  • Fraction of Discarded Compute: $$ \text{Waste} = 1 - \frac{1}{c} = \frac{c - 1}{c} $$

If $c = 1.25$, we expect to test $1.25$ proposals per accepted sample (a modest $20\%$ overhead). But if $c = 10$, we must generate and evaluate $10$ proposals on average per output variate, wasting $90\%$ of our runtime. Minimizing $c$ is the primary engineering objective in acceptance-rejection design.

2. Optimal Envelope Design and Efficiency (Lab 1)

The theoretical minimum bounding scalar $c$ for a chosen proposal density $q(x)$ is given by the supremum of their ratio across the entire target domain:

$$ c^* = \sup_{x \in \text{supp}(p)} \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} $$

Case Study: Sampling the Beta Family with Uniform Proposals

The standard Beta distribution with shape parameters $\alpha, \beta > 1$ is defined on $x \in [0, 1]$:

$$ p(x) = \frac{1}{\text{B}(\alpha, \beta)} x^{\alpha - 1} (1 - x)^{\beta - 1}, \quad \text{B}(\alpha, \beta) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha)\Gamma(\beta)}{\Gamma(\alpha + \beta)} $$

If we select a standard uniform proposal $q(x) = 1$ on $[0, 1]$, the ratio is simply the density itself: $r(x) = p(x)/1 = p(x)$. To find the supremum $c^*$, we locate the mode of the distribution by setting the derivative of the log-density to zero:

$$ \frac{d}{dx} \ln p(x) = \frac{\alpha - 1}{x} - \frac{\beta - 1}{1 - x} = 0 \implies x^* = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} $$

Substituting the modal location $x^*$ into $p(x)$ yields the exact optimal envelope height:

$$ c^* = \frac{1}{\text{B}(\alpha, \beta)} \left( \frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} \right)^{\alpha - 1} \left( \frac{\beta - 1}{\alpha + \beta - 2} \right)^{\beta - 1} $$

When $\alpha = 2$ and $\beta = 5$:

$$ x^* = \frac{2 - 1}{2 + 5 - 2} = \frac{1}{5} = 0.2 $$

$$ c^* = \frac{\Gamma(7)}{\Gamma(2)\Gamma(5)} (0.2)^1 (0.8)^4 = \frac{720}{1 \cdot 24} (0.2)(0.4096) = 30 \times 0.08192 = 2.4576 $$

Thus, our optimal uniform envelope requires $c = 2.4576$, yielding an acceptance probability of $1 / 2.4576 \approx 40.69\%$.

However, as $\alpha$ and $\beta$ grow large (e.g., $\alpha = 8, \beta = 8$), the Beta distribution concentrates into a narrow bell-like spike around $x = 0.5$. The peak height $p(0.5)$ escalates rapidly, driving $c^* \gg 4$ and causing over $75\%$ of uniform proposals to be rejected. Under such conditions, a uniform proposal is inefficient; an engineer should switch to a triangular or truncated Gaussian proposal envelope.

3. The Tail Supremum Rule: When Rejection Sampling Catastrophically Fails (Lab 2)

A common pitfall in Monte Carlo simulation is selecting an intuitive proposal distribution that inadvertently violates the mathematical precondition of the method.

The Tail Dominance Theorem

An acceptance-rejection envelope is valid if and only if the ratio $\frac{p(x)}{q(x)}$ remains strictly bounded across the entire support of $p(x)$:

$$ \sup_{x \in \text{supp}(p)} \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} < \infty $$

This requires that the proposal tail $q(x)$ must decay at least as slowly as, or slower than, the target tail $p(x)$ as $|x| \to \infty$.

If the proposal distribution has lighter tails than the target distribution, the ratio explodes in the tails:

$$ \lim_{|x| \to \infty} \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \infty \implies c = \infty $$

When $c = \infty$, the acceptance probability collapses to $1/c = 0$, rendering the method invalid.

The Fatal Trap: Sampling a Cauchy Target with a Gaussian Proposal

Suppose an engineer attempts to sample a standard Cauchy distribution (which models resonant vibrations and extreme asset returns) using a standard Normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ as the proposal:

  • Target (Cauchy): $p(x) = \frac{1}{\pi (1 + x^2)} \propto x^{-2}$ (polynomial heavy tail).
  • Proposal (Gaussian): $q(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2 / 2}$ (exponential light tail).

Evaluating the ratio $r(x)$:

$$ r(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\frac{1}{\pi(1 + x^2)}}{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \frac{e^{x^2/2}}{1 + x^2} $$

As $x \to \infty$, the exponential numerator $e^{x^2/2}$ grows incomparably faster than the quadratic denominator $1 + x^2$. Hence:

$$ \lim_{x \to \infty} r(x) = \infty $$

There is no finite constant $c$ that can cover the Cauchy distribution with a Gaussian proposal. If an engineer attempts to force this simulation on a computer by setting an arbitrary cap $c = 100$, the sampler will truncate the heavy tails, catastrophically underestimating extreme failure risks.

The Correct Pairing: Sampling Half-Normal with an Exponential Proposal

Conversely, consider sampling the positive half of a standard normal distribution (Half-Normal $X \sim \mathcal{N}^+(0, 1)$) using an Exponential proposal $q(x) = \lambda e^{-\lambda x}$ on $x \ge 0$:

$$ p(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} e^{-x^2/2}, \quad x \ge 0 $$

The ratio function is:

$$ r(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi} e^{-x^2/2}}{\lambda e^{-\lambda x}} = \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\lambda} \exp\left( \lambda x - \frac{x^2}{2} \right) $$

To find the supremum, differentiate the exponent $g(x) = \lambda x - \frac{x^2}{2}$:

$$ g'(x) = \lambda - x = 0 \implies x^* = \lambda $$

Evaluating $r(x)$ at its maximum:

$$ r(x^*) = \frac{\sqrt{2/\pi}}{\lambda} \exp\left( \frac{\lambda^2}{2} \right) $$

We now minimize this bound with respect to the proposal parameter $\lambda$:

$$ \frac{d}{d\lambda} \left[ \frac{e^{\lambda^2/2}}{\lambda} \right] = \frac{\lambda^2 e^{\lambda^2/2} - e^{\lambda^2/2}}{\lambda^2} = \frac{e^{\lambda^2/2}(\lambda^2 - 1)}{\lambda^2} = 0 \implies \lambda^* = 1 $$

With $\lambda = 1$, the optimal bounding multiplier is:

$$ c^* = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} e^{1/2} = \sqrt{\frac{2e}{\pi}} \approx 1.3155 $$

Because the exponential tail ($e^{-x}$) decays much more slowly than the Gaussian tail ($e^{-x^2/2}$), $r(x) \to 0$ as $x \to \infty$. The ratio is well-behaved, bounded, and produces a highly efficient $76.0\%$ acceptance rate.

4. Computational Acceleration: The Marsaglia Squeeze Technique (Lab 3)

Even when an acceptance-rejection sampler is mathematically optimal with a tight $c \approx 1.2$, a hidden computational bottleneck remains: evaluating the target density $p(Y)$.

In engineering simulations, $p(y)$ frequently involves computationally expensive transcendental operations—such as $\exp(-y^2/2)$, power exponents $y^{\alpha-1}$, or numerical evaluation of Gamma functions $\Gamma(\alpha)$. Computing these transcendentals on hundreds of millions of Monte Carlo particles consumes massive CPU clock cycles.

In 1968, mathematician George Marsaglia introduced an ingenious optimization known as The Squeeze Method.

The Three-Layer Sandwich

We introduce an easily computed lower bounding function $s(x)$—typically a cheap low-order polynomial or piecewise linear segment—that lies strictly beneath the target density:

$$ s(x) \le p(x) \le c \cdot q(x), \quad \forall x \in \text{supp}(p) $$
The Three-Layer Sandwich: Marsaglia Squeeze Architecture

By enclosing target density $p(x)$ between an inexpensive lower polynomial bound $s(x)$ and upper envelope $c \cdot q(x)$, candidates falling below $s(x)$ are accepted immediately without calculating transcendental functions ($\exp, \ln, \Gamma$).

● Step 1 (Fast-Path): If $V \le s(Y)$, accept immediately without computing transcendental $\exp(-Y^2/2)$.
● Step 2 (Boundary-Path): Only if $V > s(Y)$ do we evaluate exact $p(Y)$ to accept or reject.

The Accelerated Squeeze Algorithm

  1. Generate proposal $Y \sim q(y)$ and vertical test variable $V \sim \mathcal{U}(0, c \cdot q(Y))$.
  2. Fast Pre-Acceptance:
    • If $V \le s(Y)$, ACCEPT $X = Y$ IMMEDIATELY.
    • Result: Not a single transcendental function ($\exp$, $\ln$, $\sin$) is computed!
  3. Slow Boundary Test:
    • Only if $V > s(Y)$ do we pay the CPU cost to evaluate the exact $p(Y)$.
    • If $V \le p(Y)$, accept $X = Y$.
    • Otherwise, reject and repeat.

In high-performance generators (such as the Marsaglia-Tsang Gamma generator), the squeeze function $s(x)$ is so tight that over $95\%$ of all accepted variates are accepted in the fast path, delivering a $4\times$ to $8\times$ speedup in total throughput.

5. Civil & Transportation Case Study: Freeway Vehicle Time Headways

In microscopic traffic simulation models (e.g., SUMO, VISSIM, or corridor capacity digital twins), an essential stochastic input is the vehicle time headway $h_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$, defined as the time elapsed between successive vehicles passing a roadside inductive loop detector.

Under free-flowing traffic, vehicle arrivals approximate a memoryless Poisson process with exponential headways. However, under medium-to-heavy freeway flow conditions, two physical phenomena emerge:

  1. Safety Clearance Threshold: Due to physical vehicle length and driver perception-reaction limits, headways cannot drop below a minimum following threshold $\tau_0 \approx 0.5\text{s}$ to $0.7\text{s}$.
  2. Platooning Dynamics: Following vehicles get trapped behind slower leaders, clustering around a modal following headway of $1.5\text{s} - 2.5\text{s}$, before tapering into a free-flowing tail.

Transportation engineering universally models this using the Shifted Pearson Type III (Shifted Gamma) Distribution:

$$ p(h) = \frac{\lambda^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)} (h - \tau_0)^{\alpha - 1} e^{-\lambda (h - \tau_0)}, \quad h \ge \tau_0 $$
Physical Headway Regimes: Shifted Pearson Type III Density

Freeway vehicle time headways $h = t_i - t_{i-1}$ exhibiting the physical minimum following clearance $\tau_0 = 0.60\text{s}$, dense platoon clustering ($1.5\text{s} - 2.2\text{s}$), and the free-flowing exponential tail.

Why Inversion Fails

The cumulative distribution function is the regularized lower incomplete Gamma function:

$$ F(h) = P\left(\alpha, \lambda(h - \tau_0)\right) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha)} \int_0^{\lambda(h - \tau_0)} t^{\alpha - 1} e^{-t} dt $$

When $\alpha$ is a non-integer (e.g., empirical field fits yield $\alpha = 2.45$), $F(h)$ has no closed-form algebraic inverse. Numerical root-finding via Newton-Raphson across millions of vehicle arrivals in a multi-lane corridor simulation is computationally slow. Acceptance-Rejection provides an exact, vectorized alternative.

Acceptance-Rejection Calibration

Let $x = h - \tau_0 \ge 0$. Calibrating to empirical loop detector measurements from an urban freeway:

  • Minimum following headway: $\tau_0 = 0.60\text{ s}$
  • Shape parameter: $\alpha = 2.40$
  • Scale parameter: $\lambda = 1.25\text{ s}^{-1}$

We choose a Shifted Exponential proposal $q(x) = \beta e^{-\beta x}$. By matching the exponential decay rate to optimize envelope tightness, we select $\beta = \lambda / \alpha = 1.25 / 2.40 \approx 0.5208\text{ s}^{-1}$.

The ratio function is:

$$ r(x) = \frac{p(x)}{q(x)} = \frac{\lambda^\alpha}{\beta \Gamma(\alpha)} x^{\alpha - 1} e^{-(\lambda - \beta)x} $$

Differentiating with respect to $x$ reveals that the maximum occurs at:

$$ x^* = \frac{\alpha - 1}{\lambda - \beta} = \frac{2.40 - 1}{1.25 - 0.5208} = \frac{1.40}{0.7292} \approx 1.92\text{ s} $$

Evaluating $r(x^*)$ yields the optimal envelope multiplier:

$$ c^* = \frac{(1.25)^{2.40}}{0.5208 \cdot \Gamma(2.40)} (1.92)^{1.40} e^{-1.40} \approx 1.218 $$

The theoretical acceptance efficiency is:

$$ \eta = \frac{1}{c^*} = \frac{1}{1.218} \approx 82.1\% $$

Over $82\%$ of all candidate vehicle headways are accepted on the very first draw!

High-Throughput Vectorized Python Implementation

The following production-ready script implements vectorized Acceptance-Rejection sampling using NumPy, benchmarking its throughput against SciPy’s standard numeric inversion:

import numpy as np
import scipy.special as sp
import time

class FreewayHeadwaySampler:
    """
    High-performance vectorized Acceptance-Rejection sampler for
    Shifted Pearson Type III (Gamma) vehicle time headways.
    """
    def __init__(self, tau0=0.60, alpha=2.40, lam=1.25):
        self.tau0 = tau0          # Minimum safe headway (seconds)
        self.alpha = alpha        # Shape parameter
        self.lam = lam            # Scale parameter (1/seconds)
        
        # Optimal proposal parameter beta for shifted exponential
        self.beta = lam / alpha
        
        # Mode of the ratio r(x) = p(x) / q(x)
        self.x_star = (alpha - 1.0) / (lam - self.beta)
        
        # Compute exact supremum c*
        gamma_alpha = sp.gamma(alpha)
        num = (lam ** alpha) * (self.x_star ** (alpha - 1.0)) * np.exp(-(lam - self.beta) * self.x_star)
        den = self.beta * gamma_alpha
        self.c = num / den
        self.efficiency = 1.0 / self.c
        
        print(f"[Init] Bounding constant c: {self.c:.4f} (Theoretical Efficiency: {self.efficiency*100:.1f}%)")

    def target_p(self, x):
        """Unshifted Gamma density p(x) for x >= 0."""
        return (self.lam ** self.alpha / sp.gamma(self.alpha)) * (x ** (self.alpha - 1.0)) * np.exp(-self.lam * x)

    def proposal_q(self, x):
        """Exponential proposal density q(x)."""
        return self.beta * np.exp(-self.beta * x)

    def sample_vectorized(self, n_samples=1_000_000, batch_size=None):
        """
        Generates n_samples accepted vehicle headways using vectorized batching.
        """
        if batch_size is None:
            # Oversample slightly based on expected rejection rate + 4 sigma
            batch_size = int(n_samples * self.c * 1.08)
            
        accepted = []
        total_proposals = 0
        
        while len(accepted) < n_samples:
            # 1. Propose candidates Y ~ Exp(beta) via Inverse Transform
            u_prop = np.random.uniform(1e-9, 1.0, size=batch_size)
            y_cand = -np.log(u_prop) / self.beta
            
            # 2. Generate vertical test coordinates U ~ U(0, 1)
            u_test = np.random.uniform(0.0, 1.0, size=batch_size)
            
            # 3. Acceptance threshold: U <= p(Y) / (c * q(Y))
            p_val = self.target_p(y_cand)
            envelope_val = self.c * self.proposal_q(y_cand)
            accept_mask = u_test <= (p_val / envelope_val)
            
            # 4. Filter accepted candidates
            valid_y = y_cand[accept_mask]
            accepted.extend(valid_y)
            total_proposals += batch_size
            
        # Truncate to exact requested count and apply physical shift tau_0
        final_headways = np.array(accepted[:n_samples]) + self.tau0
        emp_efficiency = n_samples / total_proposals
        return final_headways, emp_efficiency

# Benchmark
if __name__ == "__main__":
    sampler = FreewayHeadwaySampler(tau0=0.60, alpha=2.40, lam=1.25)
    
    t0 = time.perf_counter()
    headways, emp_eff = sampler.sample_vectorized(n_samples=500_000)
    t1 = time.perf_counter()
    
    print(f"[Done] Generated {len(headways):,} headways in {t1 - t0:.3f} s")
    print(f"[Stat] Empirical Efficiency: {emp_eff*100:.2f}%")
    print(f"[Stat] Mean Headway: {np.mean(headways):.3f} s (Theory: {0.60 + 2.40/1.25:.3f} s)")
    print(f"[Stat] Min Headway:  {np.min(headways):.3f} s (Lower bound tau0: 0.60 s)")

6. Synthesis: Algorithm Selection Matrix & Seminal References

Choosing the appropriate non-uniform sampling method depends on distribution analytical properties, dimensional complexity, and simulation scale:

Sampling Paradigm Primary Requirements Efficiency Dimensional Scalability Primary Use Cases
Inverse Transform Method Analytical or fast numerical inverse CDF $F^{-1}(u)$ 100% (Zero waste) Univariate only ($d = 1$) Exponential, Weibull, Pareto, Cauchy, Logistics
Standard Acceptance-Rejection Known PDF $p(x)$ and majorizing envelope $c \cdot q(x)$ $1/c$ (Typically $40\% - 85\%$) Low dimensions ($d \le 3$) Beta, Gamma, Truncated Normal, Empirical Densities
Marsaglia Squeeze Technique Bounding sandwich $s(x) \le p(x) \le c q(x)$ $1/c$ ($>90\%$ fast-path bypass) Low dimensions ($d \le 3$) High-throughput micro-simulations, GPU particles
