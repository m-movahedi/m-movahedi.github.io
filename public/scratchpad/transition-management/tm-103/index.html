103: Disruptive Innovation & The S-Curve

June 16, 2026

    While the Multi-Level Perspective (MLP) provides a macro-level framework for understanding societal transitions, we must also examine the micro-economic mechanics of how specific technologies evolve and ultimately overthrow incumbent market leaders. To understand this, transition management relies heavily on two foundational theories from innovation economics: Richard Foster’s Technology S-Curve and Clayton Christensen’s theory of Disruptive Innovation.

    Together, these frameworks explain a perplexing historical paradox: why do outstanding, well-managed companies—organizations that listen carefully to their customers and invest heavily in research and development—consistently lose their market dominance when faced with certain types of technological change?

    The Technology S-Curve (The Technology Life Cycle)

    The evolution of any given technology rarely follows a linear path. Instead, as formalized by Richard Foster, technological performance improves along an “S-Curve” relative to the amount of research and development effort (or time) invested. This life cycle is characterized by three distinct phases.

    During the initial Era of Ferment (the bottom of the ‘S’), progress is incredibly slow. The technology is nascent, scientists and engineers are struggling to overcome fundamental physical or architectural bottlenecks, and financial returns on R&D investment are abysmal. However, once a dominant design emerges and the core bottlenecks are resolved, the technology enters the Takeoff Phase (the steep middle of the ‘S’). Here, progress explodes. Minor R&D investments yield massive, exponential improvements in performance, cost-efficiency, and reliability.

    Finally, the technology hits the Maturity Phase (the top of the ‘S’). It approaches its absolute physical or thermodynamic limits. In this phase, extracting even marginal improvements requires exorbitant amounts of capital and effort. The regime is ripe for substitution.

    Suggested Reading:

    • Innovation: The Attacker’s Advantage by Richard N. Foster (1986). This classic text introduces the concept of the technology S-curve and explains how attackers use new technological curves to defeat established defenders.

    Sustaining vs. Disruptive Innovation

    Incumbent companies excel at Sustaining Innovation. They listen to their best customers and make their existing products incrementally better, faster, and more profitable. They move predictably up their established S-curve.

    Disruptive Innovation, conversely, usually enters the market offering lower absolute performance in the metrics traditional customers value. However, the disruptive product is typically cheaper, simpler, smaller, or more accessible. Personal computers disrupted minicomputers not by being more powerful, but by being affordable enough for individuals to own.

    Suggested Reading:

    • The Innovator’s Dilemma by Clayton M. Christensen (1997). The seminal book outlining why great companies fail and introducing the mechanics of disruptive innovation.

    How Disruptors Win from Below

    Disruptors start at the low end of the market or in entirely new market segments that incumbents ignore. Because these segments are low-margin, incumbents are perfectly happy to surrender them to focus on their high-margin premium customers.

    But the disruptor doesn’t stay at the bottom. Supported by a steeper new S-curve, they rapidly improve their product. By the time the disruptor’s quality meets the basic performance threshold demanded by the mainstream market, they still possess their fundamental cost and accessibility advantages. The incumbent, trapped on a mature, flat S-curve and burdened by heavy overhead cost structures, is rapidly outcompeted. The disruptor takes over the market, and the transition is complete.
